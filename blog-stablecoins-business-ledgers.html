Stablecoins Move From Trading Floors to Company Ledgers

📅 August 15, 2026 · ✍️ CoinvestAI Editorial Desk · ⏱️ 7 min read

For most of their history, stablecoins were a trading tool: a way to park crypto profits in a dollar-pegged token between trades. In 2026, that framing is out of date. A growing share of stablecoin activity is now genuine commercial payments — invoices, payroll, supplier settlements and treasury transfers — moving through corporate finance departments rather than exchange order books.

The shift shows up in the numbers. McKinsey and Artemis Analytics estimate that stablecoin payment volume reflecting real commerce, stripped of trading, arbitrage and bot activity, reached roughly $390 billion in 2025, more than double the year before. Business-to-business stablecoin payments alone climbed to about $221 billion last year, and McKinsey projects that figure could pass $1 trillion by 2030 if current adoption curves hold.

Why Now: The regulatory fog has lifted

The single biggest reason enterprises are willing to touch stablecoins is that the legal ground underneath them has stopped shifting. The GENIUS Act, signed into US law in July 2025, gave stablecoin issuers their first federal framework covering reserves, licensing and redemption. In the EU, the Markets in Crypto-Assets regulation is now fully in force, setting rules for reserve composition and disclosure. Hong Kong has enacted its own Stablecoin Bill. For finance teams that spent years unable to get a straight answer from compliance on whether stablecoins were even permissible, that ambiguity is largely gone.

With the legal question mostly settled, the remaining work is operational: connecting stablecoin rails to existing accounting systems, banking relationships and audit processes, rather than resolving whether the activity is allowed at all.

Who's Building It: Card networks and fintechs in production

The infrastructure race is no longer theoretical. Visa has rolled out USDC settlement for card issuers, letting transactions settle in a stablecoin rather than waiting on traditional banking rails. Mastercard's roughly $1.8 billion acquisition of stablecoin payments infrastructure firm BVNK, announced in March, signals that the major networks see stablecoin rails as core business rather than a side experiment. Stripe Treasury, meanwhile, processed over $220 million in stablecoin payments across more than 70 countries within weeks of launching the product, using Polygon, Ethereum and Base.

Polygon's own payment-focused projects processed close to $10 billion in transaction volume in the first half of 2026 alone — already ahead of everything the network handled across all of 2025. That kind of trajectory is why treasury teams that were watching from the sidelines a year ago are now running pilot programs of their own.

Where It's Landing First: Cross-border B2B & payroll

Stablecoins aren't displacing everyday consumer payments yet — they're solving a narrower, more painful problem first: moving dollars across borders quickly and cheaply. Cross-border business payments have traditionally run through correspondent banking networks that are slow, expensive and controlled by a handful of large banks. Industry data puts the addressable market for stablecoin cross-border payments in non-G20 economies alone at roughly $17.9 trillion, which explains why infrastructure providers are concentrating effort there rather than in domestic retail.

The Gap To Watch: Raw on-chain stablecoin transfer volume is enormous — in the tens of trillions of dollars — but analysts caution that most of it is trading, internal transfers between wallets, and automated bot activity rather than genuine commerce. The $390 billion "real payments" figure is the adjusted number that strips this out, and it's the one worth tracking if the question is business adoption rather than crypto trading activity.

What Could Slow It Down: Trust & fragmentation

None of this is a straight line upward. Consumer trust in holding a dollar-pegged token instead of a bank deposit remains uneven, and the stablecoin market itself is fragmented across multiple competing tokens and blockchains with different liquidity and compliance profiles. Banks also have an obvious incentive to slow a technology that threatens to disintermediate their own payment rails, and several large institutions are responding by building their own stablecoin products rather than ceding the category entirely.

Compliance also remains a live operational cost, not a solved problem. Sanctions screening, transaction monitoring and local regulatory requirements differ market by market, and payment providers are investing heavily in automated compliance tooling to keep pace as volumes grow.

The Takeaway: 2026 is the year of financial utility

What's notable about this cycle isn't a price chart — it's that the conversation has moved from exchanges to CFOs. Stablecoins are being adopted the way most financial infrastructure is: quietly, by businesses chasing faster settlement and lower fees, well before most consumers notice the rail underneath their payment has changed. If the current trajectory holds, the next few years will be less about whether stablecoins work as payments infrastructure, and more about which networks, issuers and banks end up owning the rails.
