Most backtests are wrong, and they are wrong in a specific, predictable direction: they overstate. A strategy that looks excellent in simulation and disappoints in production has usually not been unlucky — it has been tested with a methodology whose errors all point the same way. This problem is worse for AI-generated signals than for rule-based ones, because machine learning makes it trivially cheap to try thousands of configurations, and every configuration tried is an opportunity to find one that fit the past by chance. Backtesting is therefore not a performance-measurement exercise; it is a hypothesis-falsification exercise, and the guide below is organized around that reframe: what biases to eliminate first, how to structure validation so that overfitting is detectable, and how to report results in a way that survives scrutiny.

1. Why AI Strategies Break Backtesting Assumptions

Classical backtesting methodology was developed for strategies with a small number of hand-specified parameters. A moving-average crossover has two numbers. Testing a thousand variants of it is feasible, and correcting for that multiplicity is straightforward. Machine learning changes the arithmetic in four ways:

  • Search space explodes. Feature selection, hyperparameter tuning, architecture choice, and threshold selection multiply into an effective trial count in the thousands or more — most of which are never explicitly logged. The multiplicity correction must account for trials actually performed, not trials reported.
  • Flexible models fit noise. A gradient-boosted tree or neural network will find structure in random data given enough capacity. In-sample fit is therefore nearly uninformative about generalization, and the gap between in-sample and out-of-sample performance is itself the primary diagnostic.
  • Feature engineering leaks. Normalization, imputation, scaling, and target encoding computed over the full dataset embed future information into every historical observation. This is the most common and most damaging error in ML backtesting, and it is invisible in the results.
  • Non-stationarity compounds. Markets change regime. A model tuned on one volatility or correlation environment does not transfer, and no amount of clever validation fixes a relationship that has ceased to exist.
Key Insight: The right question is not "what does this strategy return in backtest?" It is "given everything I tried, what is the probability that this particular configuration's performance is distinguishable from the best of N random strategies?" That reframing turns backtesting into a multiple-hypothesis-testing problem, which has established statistical answers — and those answers are almost always more pessimistic than the naive result.

2. The Bias Taxonomy — Eliminate These First

Every bias below inflates backtested performance. Addressing them in order matters, because a beautiful validation design on biased data produces confident nonsense.

2.1 Look-ahead bias

Using information at time t that was not actually available until after t. Sources include: restated fundamentals (a company's originally-reported figures differ from later restatements, and only the restated version is in most datasets), economic data released with a lag and subsequently revised, index membership applied retrospectively (a stock is in the index today, so it appears in the historical index), closing-price features used to generate a signal that trades at that same close, and corporate-action adjustments applied with hindsight. The mitigation is a strict point-in-time data layer — see section 3.

2.2 Survivorship bias

Testing on the set of securities that exist today excludes everything that was delisted, acquired, or bankrupt. In equities this can materially overstate long-only returns, because the losers have been removed from the sample. In crypto the effect is far larger: a very high proportion of listed tokens have gone to zero or lost liquidity entirely, and any universe constructed from currently-trading pairs is severely biased. Use a database that retains delisted history, and reconstruct the investable universe as it was at each point in time.

2.3 Selection and data-snooping bias

Choosing the strategy, the parameters, or the test period after seeing results. Subtler variants: selecting the backtest window that happens to contain favorable regimes, dropping a bad year as an "outlier," or iterating until the equity curve looks acceptable. Every iteration you perform and do not report increases the selection bias in what you do report. The discipline is to log every trial — configuration, period, and result — in a registry, and to correct statistics for the total number of trials.

2.4 Transaction-cost and liquidity bias

Assuming execution at observed prices with no cost, no impact, and unlimited size. See section 8; this alone routinely converts apparently profitable high-turnover strategies into losers.

2.5 Capacity bias

Backtesting a strategy at a size the market cannot absorb. A signal that works on a notional amount equal to a few percent of average daily volume may be untradeable at institutional size, and the degradation is nonlinear.

2.6 Regime and sample-period bias

A short test window may contain only one volatility or correlation regime. Results from a low-volatility period do not extrapolate. Report performance segmented by regime, and require the test period to span at least one stress episode.

2.7 Model-implementation bias

The backtest code differs from what would trade live: fills assumed at bid-ask mid rather than at the executable side, orders assumed fully filled, no queue position modeling, no partial fills, no rejections, no latency. Validate the simulator against real execution data wherever possible.

3. Data Hygiene: Point-in-Time Is Non-Negotiable

A point-in-time (PIT) data layer stores every observation together with the timestamp at which it became known, not merely the timestamp to which it refers. This is the single highest-leverage infrastructure investment in quantitative research, and retrofitting it after a strategy is developed is close to impossible because the contamination is already embedded in the results.

  • Two timestamps per record. as_of_date (the period the datum describes) and knowledge_date (when it became available). Queries filter on knowledge_date ≤ decision time.
  • Never overwrite. Revisions are appended, not replaced. Original filings, revised figures, and restatements all persist.
  • Universe reconstruction. Index constituents, listing status, liquidity screens, and sector classifications as they were at each date, including delisted securities with their terminal outcomes.
  • Corporate actions applied as-of. Splits, dividends, and ticker changes applied on their effective dates using information available then.
  • Feature pipeline determinism. Every transformation must be reproducible from PIT data alone. Fit all scaling, imputation, and encoding parameters on training folds only, and apply them to later folds — never fit on the full dataset.
  • Data vendor provenance and versioning. Vendor datasets change silently. Version every snapshot used and record which version produced which result.

For the feature-store patterns that make this operationally practical, see our guide to real-time risk scoring pipelines, which faces the identical point-in-time discipline under latency constraints.

4. Labeling: Turning Forecasts into Testable Decisions

Labeling is where most ML trading research is decided, and it is treated as an afterthought more often than any other step. A label defines what "success" means for a single observation, and a poorly chosen label makes the model accurate about something nobody trades on.

4.1 Fixed-horizon returns

The default: label as the sign or magnitude of the return over h periods. Simple and widely used, but flawed because it ignores the path. A position that suffered a large drawdown before recovering is labeled identically to one that rose smoothly, even though they have completely different risk characteristics and would be managed differently in practice.

4.2 Triple-barrier labeling

Set three boundaries from the entry point: a profit-taking barrier, a stop-loss barrier, and a time limit. The label is whichever barrier is touched first. This encodes the actual decision — you exit when either barrier is hit or when time expires — and it produces labels aligned with how the strategy will really be traded. Barrier widths should be scaled by contemporaneous volatility rather than fixed, so that labels are comparable across volatility regimes.

4.3 Volatility scaling and sample weighting

Financial observations are not independent and not identically distributed. Two adjustments matter:

  • Volatility targeting. Scale positions inversely to recent realized volatility so that each observation contributes comparable risk. Without this, high-volatility periods dominate both the label distribution and the loss function.
  • Uniqueness weighting. When labels span multiple periods, consecutive samples overlap and share information. Compute each observation's average uniqueness — the fraction of its label horizon not shared with other concurrent labels — and weight samples accordingly. Ignoring overlap inflates effective sample size and understates estimator variance.

4.4 Meta-labeling

A two-stage design that separates direction from sizing. A primary model or rule proposes the trade direction; a secondary ML model predicts the probability that the proposed trade will be profitable, and that probability determines position size or whether to trade at all. The practical benefit is substantial: primary signals often have poor hit rates but good directional information in specific conditions, and the meta-model learns those conditions. It also decouples "when is the signal reliable?" from "what does the signal say?", which makes each component separately diagnosable.

4.5 Leakage in labeling

Triple-barrier labels look forward by construction — that is their purpose — which means the label horizon defines an embargo requirement for cross-validation. Any sample whose label window overlaps a test period must be excluded from training on that test period. This is exactly what purged cross-validation handles, in the next section.

5. Cross-Validation for Financial Sequences

Standard K-fold cross-validation is invalid on financial time series for two reasons: it shuffles, destroying temporal order, and it allows overlapping label windows to appear in both train and test folds, which leaks future information into training.

5.1 Purged K-fold with embargo

Two corrections restore validity:

  • Purging. Remove from the training set any observation whose label window overlaps the test window. For a label horizon of h periods, this removes a band of width h around each test fold.
  • Embargo. Additionally remove a small fraction of observations immediately after each test window. This guards against the leakage that persists through serial correlation and through features computed on rolling windows that straddle the boundary. A common choice is a few percent of total samples.

5.2 Combinatorial purged cross-validation

Instead of a single train/test split per fold, generate many combinations of test folds, producing a larger number of out-of-sample paths. The output is a distribution of out-of-sample performance rather than a point estimate, which lets you assess the strategy's variance across periods and detect configurations that work only in specific windows. The cost is compute; the benefit is that a single favorable split no longer determines your conclusion.

5.3 Anchored versus rolling windows

  • Rolling (sliding) window. Fixed-size training window that advances with time. Adapts faster to regime change; discards old information that may still be relevant.
  • Expanding (anchored) window. Training window grows from a fixed start. Uses all available history; increasingly weighted toward old regimes, and more exposed to structural breaks.

Neither is universally correct. The choice should follow from how fast the underlying relationship decays, which is itself an empirical question — test both and compare out-of-sample stability, not just average performance. For signals driven by slow structural factors, expanding windows often win; for microstructure signals, short rolling windows usually do.

5.4 Retraining cadence

Retraining frequency should match signal decay, not convenience. Retrain too often and you chase noise; too rarely and you trade a stale model through a regime change. Measure the decay of your signal's predictive power directly — how quickly out-of-sample performance degrades as the model ages — and set the cadence from that curve.

6. Walk-Forward Analysis: Design Choices That Matter

Walk-forward analysis simulates the actual research-to-production loop: optimize on a window, trade on the following unseen window, roll forward, repeat. It is more honest than a single split because it produces many out-of-sample periods and exposes instability across them. The design choices, however, determine whether the result means anything.

  • Number of windows. Too few windows and the out-of-sample distribution is estimated from a handful of points, which tells you nothing about variance. Aim for enough windows that you can compute a meaningful distribution of out-of-sample Sharpe ratios, and report the spread, not just the mean.
  • Train-to-test ratio. A very long training window relative to the test window produces stable but stale models; a short one produces adaptive but noisy models. There is no universal optimum — test several ratios and report sensitivity. A strategy whose conclusion flips with the ratio is not validated.
  • What is re-optimized. If hyperparameters are re-tuned in each window using the test period's data, you have leaked. Re-optimization must use only information available up to the window boundary.
  • Window boundaries and regime alignment. Boundaries that happen to fall at regime changes produce misleading results in both directions. Examine where boundaries fall relative to volatility regimes rather than accepting the default grid.
  • Stitching. Concatenating out-of-sample windows into a single equity curve is standard, but be aware that this assumes capital continuity across windows and hides the fact that the model was different in each. Report both the stitched curve and the per-window statistics.
Diagnostic worth adding: Plot out-of-sample performance per window against in-sample performance for the corresponding training window. A consistent, moderate gap is normal and healthy. A gap that widens over time indicates a decaying relationship; in-sample performance that improves while out-of-sample deteriorates is the classic overfitting signature and should stop the project.

7. Overfitting Detection: Deflated Sharpe, PBO, and MTL

This is the section most backtesting guides omit, and it is the one that determines whether your result means anything. Three tools convert "good backtest" into a testable statistical claim.

7.1 Deflated Sharpe Ratio

The observed Sharpe ratio is an upward-biased estimate of true skill when the strategy was selected from many trials, and it is also affected by non-normal return distributions. The deflated Sharpe ratio adjusts the observed value for both: the number of independent trials performed, and the skewness and excess kurtosis of the return series. The output is a probability that the true Sharpe ratio exceeds zero after accounting for selection.

Practical requirements: you must know or estimate the number of trials, and you must estimate the variance of Sharpe ratios across those trials. This is why a trial registry (section 2.3) is not bureaucracy — without it the deflation cannot be computed honestly. A related and simpler tool is the haircut Sharpe ratio, which applies a reduction based on the selection procedure's aggressiveness.

7.2 Probability of Backtest Overfitting

The combinatorially-symmetric cross-validation approach estimates the probability that the configuration selected as best in-sample will perform below the median out-of-sample. The procedure: split the data into many blocks, form all combinations of in-sample and out-of-sample block sets, select the best configuration on each in-sample set, and measure where that configuration ranks on the corresponding out-of-sample set. If in-sample winners systematically land in the out-of-sample bottom half, the selection process is overfitting regardless of how good the reported numbers look.

This is arguably the single most useful overfitting diagnostic available, because it tests the selection process rather than the selected strategy — and the selection process is where the error actually lives.

7.3 Minimum Backtest Length

Given a target Sharpe ratio, the number of trials performed, and the return distribution's skew and kurtosis, there is a minimum sample length below which you cannot distinguish skill from luck at a given confidence level. Compute it before interpreting results. If your available history is shorter than the minimum backtest length for the number of configurations you tried, the honest conclusion is that the test is inconclusive — not that the strategy works.

7.4 Multiple-testing corrections for strategy families

When evaluating a family of strategies, bootstrap-based procedures such as White's Reality Check and Hansen's Superior Predictive Ability test assess whether the best performer beats a benchmark after accounting for the search. These are more conservative than naive comparisons and are appropriate whenever you are selecting among many candidates.

7.5 Simple diagnostics that catch most overfitting

  • Parameter sensitivity. Perturb every parameter slightly. A strategy that works only at one exact value is fitting noise. Robust strategies show a broad plateau, not a sharp peak.
  • In-sample versus out-of-sample ratio. Large divergence is the primary signature. Report both always.
  • Cross-market and cross-asset replication. A relationship that exists only in one instrument and one period is suspect. Genuine structural signals usually appear, weakened, in related markets.
  • Return contribution concentration. If most of the profit comes from a handful of trades or a single episode, the result is not a repeatable edge — it is one event.
  • Complexity penalty. Compare against the simplest plausible alternative. If a twelve-feature model cannot beat a two-feature one out of sample, the extra features are noise.

8. Transaction Costs, Slippage, and Capacity

Cost modeling is where backtests most often diverge from live reality, and it is the primary reason high-turnover AI signals fail in production. Model every component explicitly.

8.1 Cost components

  • Explicit costs. Commissions, exchange and clearing fees, regulatory fees, borrow costs for short positions, and financing on leverage. These are known and should be modeled exactly, not approximated.
  • Spread cost. At minimum, assume execution at the quoted spread's executable side, not the mid. For less liquid instruments, model the spread as time-varying and correlated with volatility — spreads widen exactly when you most need to trade.
  • Market impact. The price movement caused by your own order. The empirical regularity most used is a square-root relationship: impact scales roughly with the square root of order size relative to volume, with an instrument- and regime-specific coefficient. Calibrate the coefficient from your own execution data once you have it; until then, use conservative published estimates and stress them.
  • Slippage from latency. The gap between signal computation and execution. For any strategy with intraday horizons, model a realistic delay distribution rather than assuming instantaneous fills at the signal price.
  • Opportunity and non-fill cost. Limit orders do not always fill. A backtest that assumes every limit order executes at its limit price systematically overstates performance, because the orders that would have been most profitable are the ones most likely to have been missed.

8.2 Queue and partial-fill modeling

For strategies resting orders in the book, model queue position and partial fills. The naive assumption — that a limit order fills whenever the price touches it — is optimistic to a degree that can dominate the strategy's entire edge.

8.3 Capacity analysis

Determine the notional size at which the strategy's net edge degrades to zero, and report it alongside returns. Capacity is a function of the instrument's traded volume, your participation-rate tolerance, and impact coefficients. A strategy with a small edge and enormous capacity is often more valuable than one with a large edge and negligible capacity — but only the second one looks impressive in a backtest, which is why capacity must be reported explicitly.

8.4 Cost sensitivity as a required output

Report performance at multiple cost assumptions — base, stressed, and severe. If the strategy's profitability depends on the base assumption being right, it is not robust. Also report the turnover-adjusted edge: gross return per unit of turnover, which lets you compare strategies with different trading frequencies on comparable terms.

9. Performance Metrics Beyond Return

Return alone is meaningless without its distribution and its cost. Report at minimum:

MetricWhat It Tells YouCommon Misuse
Sharpe ratioExcess return per unit of total volatilityReported without trial-count deflation or non-normality adjustment; compared across different frequencies without annualization care
Sortino ratioReturn per unit of downside deviationUsed to excuse large upside volatility that is really regime luck
Maximum drawdown and durationWorst peak-to-trough loss and time to recoveryReporting only depth; duration and the sequence of drawdowns matter more for survivability
Calmar / MARReturn relative to worst drawdownHighly sensitive to sample period; unstable on short histories
Hit rate and payoff ratioFrequency of wins and average win-to-loss sizeReported separately; the product matters, and high hit rate with poor payoff is a classic failure mode
TurnoverTrading frequency, hence cost loadOmitted entirely, which hides cost sensitivity
Tail metrics (CVaR, skew, kurtosis)Behavior in extreme outcomesIgnored until a stress episode reveals a distribution the Sharpe ratio never showed
Out-of-sample decayHow fast predictive power fades with model ageNot measured, so retraining cadence is set arbitrarily
Regime-segmented performanceBehavior across volatility and correlation environmentsAveraged across regimes, concealing that all return came from one
CapacitySize at which net edge vanishesNever reported, so a backtest at unrealistic size is treated as scalable

Two reporting habits separate credible research from marketing. First, always show the distribution of out-of-sample results across windows, not only the aggregate. Second, always state the number of configurations tried — without it, no reader can interpret any statistic you report.

10. From Backtest to Live: A Staged Validation Path

A backtest is one piece of evidence in a sequence. Skipping stages is how institutions deploy models that were never going to work.

  1. Hypothesis and economic rationale. Before any data: why should this edge exist, who is on the other side, and why has it not been arbitraged away? A strategy with no story is a pattern mined from noise until proven otherwise.
  2. Point-in-time data verification. Confirm the PIT layer, universe reconstruction, and feature-pipeline determinism before modeling. This is cheap now and impossible later.
  3. In-sample development with purged CV. Establish that the relationship exists in-sample under leak-free validation.
  4. Held-out out-of-sample period, untouched. Reserve a final period that is used exactly once, at the end. Once you look at it and iterate, it becomes in-sample. Many teams exhaust their held-out data through repeated peeking and then have nothing left to test with.
  5. Overfitting diagnostics. Deflated Sharpe, PBO, minimum backtest length, parameter sensitivity. If these fail, stop — do not proceed to paper trading to "see if it works."
  6. Paper trading / simulation on live data. Tests the pipeline end to end: data feeds, signal generation latency, order construction, and cost assumptions against live quotes. Does not test execution quality or market impact.
  7. Small live allocation. The first real test of impact, fill rates, and operational reliability. Compare realized execution against the model's cost assumptions and recalibrate — this comparison is the highest-value data you will collect.
  8. Scaled deployment with monitoring. Defined performance bounds, drift detection, kill criteria established in advance, and a documented decision owner for halting.
  9. Continuous revalidation. Scheduled out-of-time performance review, challenger models, and re-running the overfitting diagnostics as the trial count grows.

Each stage should have explicit pass criteria decided before the stage runs. Criteria set after seeing results are not criteria.

11. A Complete Backtesting Checklist

Use this before accepting any backtest result — yours or a vendor's. Every item is a place where the answer is usually "we assumed."

Data

  • Point-in-time data layer with separate as-of and knowledge timestamps
  • Delisted securities retained with terminal outcomes; universe reconstructed as-of each date
  • Restatements and revisions handled; original reported values available
  • Corporate actions applied as-of effective date
  • Feature transforms fit only on training data; no full-dataset normalization
  • Data vendor versions recorded and reproducible

Methodology

  • Purged cross-validation with embargo appropriate to label horizon
  • Walk-forward with enough windows to report a distribution, not a point
  • Untouched final held-out period, used exactly once
  • Labeling scheme aligned with actual trading decision (barrier or equivalent)
  • Sample weighting for label overlap / uniqueness
  • Volatility-scaled positions and barriers

Overfitting controls

  • Trial registry: every configuration tested, logged with results
  • Deflated or haircut Sharpe computed using actual trial count
  • Probability of backtest overfitting estimated via combinatorial CV
  • Minimum backtest length checked against available history
  • Parameter sensitivity showing a plateau rather than a peak
  • Comparison against the simplest plausible alternative

Costs and capacity

  • Explicit commissions, fees, borrow, and financing
  • Spread modeled at executable side and time-varying
  • Market impact calibrated, with square-root scaling and stress cases
  • Latency and slippage modeled with a realistic delay distribution
  • Partial fills and queue position modeled for resting orders
  • Capacity — notional at which net edge reaches zero — reported
  • Performance reported at base, stressed, and severe cost assumptions

Reporting

  • Distribution of out-of-sample results across windows, not just the mean
  • Regime-segmented performance spanning at least one stress episode
  • Drawdown depth and duration; tail metrics; turnover
  • Return-contribution concentration disclosed
  • Number of trials stated prominently
  • Economic rationale documented before results
  • Pass criteria for each validation stage fixed in advance

💬 Frequently Asked Questions

What is backtest overfitting and how do I detect it?

Backtest overfitting occurs when a strategy's apparent performance comes from having selected, out of many configurations tried, the one that best fit historical noise. It is detectable, and the most useful diagnostic tests the selection process rather than the selected strategy: combinatorially-symmetric cross-validation splits data into many blocks, selects the best configuration on each in-sample combination, and measures where that configuration ranks out-of-sample. If in-sample winners systematically land in the out-of-sample bottom half, your selection process is overfitting. Complement this with the deflated Sharpe ratio, minimum backtest length, and parameter sensitivity — a genuine edge shows a broad plateau under perturbation, while an overfit one shows a sharp peak.

Why is standard K-fold cross-validation invalid for trading models?

For two reasons. It shuffles observations, destroying the temporal ordering that financial data depends on, and it allows samples with overlapping label windows to appear in both training and test folds, which leaks future information into training. The fix is purged cross-validation with an embargo: remove from the training set every observation whose label window overlaps the test window, then additionally remove a small percentage of observations immediately after each test window to guard against leakage through serial correlation and rolling-window features. The purge width is determined by your label horizon, so labeling design and validation design are coupled.

How much do transaction costs really change backtest results?

Enough to reverse conclusions for high-turnover strategies, which is most machine-learning signals. Costs compound from four sources that are frequently modeled badly or not at all: spread cost when execution is assumed at mid rather than at the executable side, market impact which scales roughly with the square root of order size relative to volume, latency slippage between signal computation and execution, and the optimistic assumption that every limit order fills whenever price touches it. Always report performance at base, stressed, and severe cost assumptions, and report the notional capacity at which net edge reaches zero. If profitability depends on the base cost assumption being exactly right, the strategy is not robust.

What is survivorship bias and does it matter for crypto?

Survivorship bias means testing on the set of assets that still exist today, which excludes everything delisted, acquired, or bankrupt — systematically removing losers from the sample and overstating long-only returns. In equities the effect is material and well documented. In crypto it is far larger, because a very high proportion of listed tokens have gone to zero or lost liquidity entirely; a universe built from currently-trading pairs is severely biased and can turn a losing strategy into an apparently excellent one. The fix is a database that retains delisted history with terminal outcomes, and reconstruction of the investable universe as it was at each historical date rather than as it is now.

What is the deflated Sharpe ratio and why do I need the number of trials?

The observed Sharpe ratio is an upward-biased estimate of true skill whenever the strategy was selected from multiple attempts, and it is further distorted by non-normal returns. The deflated Sharpe ratio corrects for both by using the number of independent trials performed and the variance of Sharpe ratios across those trials, producing a probability that true skill exceeds zero after accounting for selection. That is why a trial registry is essential rather than bureaucratic: if you do not know how many configurations you tested, you cannot compute the correction honestly, and the headline Sharpe ratio is uninterpretable. A simpler alternative is the haircut Sharpe ratio, which reduces the observed value based on how aggressive the selection procedure was.

How long should a backtest period be?

Long enough to satisfy the minimum backtest length for your target Sharpe ratio, trial count, and return distribution — which is computable before you interpret any result and depends on skewness and kurtosis, not just on years of data. Beyond that minimum, the period must span at least one stress episode and multiple volatility regimes, because a strategy validated only in calm conditions has not been validated. If your available history is shorter than the minimum backtest length for the number of configurations you tried, the honest conclusion is that the test is inconclusive. Do not proceed to paper trading to see whether it works anyway.

Should I re-optimize parameters in each walk-forward window?

Only using information available up to that window's boundary. Re-tuning hyperparameters with any reference to the subsequent test period leaks future information and invalidates the out-of-sample claim, which is easy to do accidentally when the optimization loop is automated. Also treat the train-to-test ratio as a sensitivity parameter rather than a fixed choice: report results across several ratios, because a strategy whose conclusion flips when the ratio changes has not been validated. And measure how quickly out-of-sample performance degrades as the model ages, since that decay curve — not convention — should set your retraining cadence.

What is meta-labeling and when should I use it?

Meta-labeling separates trade direction from trade sizing. A primary model or rule proposes direction; a secondary machine-learning model predicts the probability that the proposed trade will be profitable, and that probability determines position size or whether to trade at all. Use it when your primary signal has useful directional information but an unreliable hit rate, which is common — the meta-model learns the conditions under which the signal is trustworthy. It also improves diagnosability, because 'what does the signal say' and 'when is the signal reliable' become separately testable components rather than one entangled model.
